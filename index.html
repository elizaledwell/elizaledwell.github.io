FEMA · IBM · Risk MAP Program

NFHL Print Tool — Prototyped the Nation's First Address-Based FIRMette Generator

Before this tool existed, accessing your official flood insurance rate map meant contacting FEMA's Map Service Center, waiting, or navigating cumbersome 36" × 25" paper panels. In 2012, I prototyped the NFHL Print Tool — a GIS web application that allowed any user to simply type a street address and instantly generate a legally valid FIRMette: a scaled, printable section of the official Flood Insurance Rate Map (FIRM) formatted for standard office printers.

This was a fundamental shift in how flood hazard data reached the public. The tool integrated address geocoding with the National Flood Hazard Layer (NFHL) database, dynamically clipping and rendering the correct map panel at regulatory scale, applying FEMA's required accuracy standards, and producing a PDF-ready output on demand. Built using JavaScript for the spatial UI layer and Python for data processing and validation pipelines.

Why it mattered: FIRMettes are legally accepted for all NFIP purposes — flood insurance underwriting, mortgage lending, floodplain management decisions, and LOMA applications. Democratizing on-demand digital access to this data empowered homeowners, insurance agents, lenders, and local planners in ways that paper maps never could. The capability is now a core feature of FEMA's national geospatial infrastructure, used by millions annually.

FEMA · NiyamIT · National Flood Insurance Program

Risk Rating Engine 2.0 — Modernizing the Science Behind Flood Insurance Pricing

FEMA's legacy flood insurance rating system had used a simplistic zone-based model for decades — if your property was in a flood zone, you paid a fixed rate based on that zone designation, regardless of your actual, individualized risk. Risk Rating 2.0 replaced that with a sophisticated actuarial pricing engine incorporating multiple property-level variables: first-floor height, distance to the nearest water feature, foundation type, structure characteristics, and multiple flood types (riverine, coastal, storm surge, heavy rainfall).

As the Business Architect, I led the development and transition effort — coordinating across FEMA's technology, actuarial, policy, and communications teams. This required deep technical understanding of how spatial data from the NFHL integrated with actuarial models, as well as extensive stakeholder management with state and local floodplain managers who depended on the rating system being accurate, explainable, and defensible.

Why it mattered: Risk Rating 2.0 launched October 2021 and represented the most significant transformation of NFIP pricing in the program's 50+ year history. It introduced genuine, science-based individualized risk pricing for approximately 5 million NFIP policyholders — making insurance costs more equitable and encouraging risk-informed decisions about where and how people build.

DHS · Geospatial Management Office · National Operations Center

DHS Common Operating Picture (COP) — Strategic Geospatial Situational Awareness for National Incident Command

The Common Operating Picture is the strategic geospatial platform supporting the DHS National Operations Center (NOC) — the command hub for monitoring and coordinating the federal response to incidents of national significance. As Deputy Program Manager, I lead not only the delivery of this mission-critical system but its fundamental transformation: modernizing how DHS components collect, share, and act on geospatial intelligence at the strategic level.

A centerpiece of this work has been establishing common operating data standards across DHS components. I coordinated with agencies department-wide to build a unified data foundation — ensuring every component reports off the same authoritative geospatial datasets, while preserving each component's flexibility in how they ingest, visualize, and report that data based on their distinct operational missions and granularity requirements. The result is coherent national situational awareness without imposing one-size-fits-all constraints on the components who depend on it.

I drove a strategic IT contract consolidation — bringing multiple separate support contracts under a single unified vehicle covering the COP, the Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD) program, and the Geospatial Information Infrastructure (GII). This represents an inflection point in how the Geospatial Management Office governs its full IT portfolio: reducing redundancy, improving accountability, and enabling coordinated delivery across three programs that had previously operated in silos.

To accelerate this procurement, I piloted AI-assisted acquisition using DHS's internal AI chat tool — applying it to draft procurement documents and support the evaluation of RFP responses. The experiment demonstrated that AI can meaningfully compress the timeline and analytical burden of federal acquisition, and served as a proof-of-concept for AI-augmented contracting practices across the Geospatial Management Office.

I also transformed the contractor delivery model — maturing teams from traditional waterfall execution into agile operating practices that prioritize frequent value delivery and rapid reprioritization in response to shifting leadership direction, a capability essential in the fast-moving national security environment.

Why it mattered: The NOC depends on the COP to maintain strategic awareness during the nation's most consequential events — natural disasters, border incidents, and complex homeland security operations. The data unification work means every DHS leader making decisions during a major incident is working from the same geospatial truth. The contract consolidation and agile transformation mean that infrastructure can evolve quickly when the mission demands it. And the AI procurement pilot points to a scalable future for how federal programs manage the overhead of acquisition.

Harvard Forest · Harvard University · Summer Research

Forecasting Potential Habitat of Tsuga canadensis Under Climate Change Using Bioclimatic Envelope Models

As an undergraduate summer researcher at Harvard Forest, I conducted original scientific research on the response of eastern hemlock (Tsuga canadensis) to climate change — applying bioclimatic envelope modeling to one of the most pressing questions in conservation science: how will species distributions shift as the climate changes, and can we build models reliable enough to guide policy?

My research employed a suite of species distribution models to assess how climate change would reshape the potential habitat of eastern hemlock across eastern North America. The work addressed two core scientific questions: how well do models calibrated to current climate conditions predict mid-Holocene distributions — and vice versa — and how do different climate change projections produce divergent forecasts of future habitat? I also developed methods for quantifying uncertainty in model forecasts, addressing a critical gap in how bioclimatic models communicate reliability to decision-makers.

The findings showed that climate is a strong predictor of hemlock distribution both at present and in the past, lending credibility to bioclimatic envelopes as a reasonable first approximation of species response to climatic change. Forecasts from both current and paleoclimate-calibrated models agreed: hemlock's climatic range will shift in a northeastern direction under future projections, absent dispersal limitations. This convergence across model approaches strengthened confidence in the directional signal, even amid uncertainty in magnitude.

The geospatial data processing work I contributed — organizing and programming the spatial datasets underpinning this and related modeling efforts at Harvard Forest — was credited in a peer-reviewed publication by Record et al. (2013) in Global Ecology and Biogeography, a study that tested spatial versus non-spatial species distribution models across 8,000 years of paleoclimate change.

Why it mattered: Eastern hemlock is a keystone forest species across the northeastern United States, with cascading ecological importance for stream temperatures, biodiversity, and forest structure. Reliable forecasts of its climate-driven range shift are directly actionable for conservation planners and land managers. By evaluating model reliability and quantifying forecast uncertainty, this research moved beyond simply predicting range shifts to asking: how much should we trust these predictions — a question central to evidence-based environmental policy. This work planted the scientific seeds for the AI-driven environmental modeling research Eliza pursues today.
